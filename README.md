Weil's criterion states that the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the positivity of an explicit quadratic form Q built from the primes, the pole of the zeta function, and an archimedean term. We implement Q as a matrix over a family of Gaussian test functions, validate it against the first 500 nontrivial zeros to 7 significant digits, and measure five structural properties: (1) the prime "tone system" {k·ln p, weight ln p·p^(−k/2)} is a razor-sharp critical point of positivity — shifting any length, scaling any weight in either direction, or injecting any tone at a composite location breaks positivity linearly in the perturbation; (2) the positivity margin decays exponentially with test-function support length at measured rate ≈ 22.2 ≈ πγ₁/2, the Slepian–prolate concentration constant of the first zero; (3) positivity is sustained by a relay: the archimedean part alone fails at ~ln 2, and each prime restores positivity exactly until the next prime's logarithm; (4) the deficit of each partial form is pinned to the logarithms of the missing primes, not to the geometry of the test window — the failure mode of the continuum is a portrait of the remaining primes; (5) in a pilot uniqueness test, all density-matched Cramér-random competitors and Beurling dilations fail positivity by up to six orders of magnitude relative to the true primes. We relate these observations to the Connes–Consani program and state a classification conjecture.
